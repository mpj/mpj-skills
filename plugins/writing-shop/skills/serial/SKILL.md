# serial

An agent deep in a context assumes its reader is in the same context and has read everything. Then it delivers in one block, so the reader stops a few paragraphs in with a question and has to type it underneath the whole thing. In conversation they would have said: wait, I haven't understood this yet, explain that first. This is that stop, built into the form.

**Parts.** Five to eight of them, 120 to 220 words each including the checkpoint, delivered one at a time. Inverted pyramid: part 1 carries the whole point in plain words a reader with no context can follow, and each later part adds one layer under it, a mechanism, an incident, a number. A reader who stops after any part holds a true picture at that depth. Assume they have read nothing: not the document you are explaining, not the earlier conversation, not the file you just wrote. They are arriving cold, holding a sandwich.

**Vocabulary.** Each part introduces at most three new terms or names. Each is defined in plain words at its first use, before anything leans on it, and no term is used in part N that was not grounded in parts 1 to N. The definitions are the specification, not clutter; an editor may sharpen them and may not cut them. The defining occurrence of each term is set in bold, once, and never again (the checkpoint and the ground line list their terms plain): a reader landing mid-serial scans back for the bold and finds where the word was introduced, and the ground line uses the same spelling. When a part iterates over named things, each name is bold at its first appearance in the list. Italics stay the writer's, for stress. That is the whole typographic budget; a page where everything is emphasised has no emphasis.

**The masthead.** Above every part, outside the prose and not counted as prose, a fixed frame for the reader who just landed from another tab and needs to know in one glance what this is, who it is for, and where they stand. Four slots: a **title**, two to five words, a name a reader could pick out of a list of twenty documents, never a summary and never a name with an explainer after a colon; a **standfirst**, one sentence under twenty words saying what this document is and what it is about, so plainly a stranger understands it, never a teaser or a preview; a **position line**, part N of M, the date, and who wrote it; and from part 2 on a **ground line**, "So far:" followed by the terms already grounded, a bare list of nouns, mirroring the checkpoints. The writer writes the masthead, in the voice, under the same rules as the prose. A masthead written by the relay in its own register is the first thing the reader will call machine-made, and one was.

**The checkpoint.** The last paragraph of every part, in the writer's voice: it names the terms the part introduced and invites the reader either to say continue or to ask about any of them and how they relate. Vary the wording. A checkpoint is an invitation and never a preview; a sentence telling the reader what is coming is signposting, and the tightening seat may cut it.

**Between parts.** Show part 1 and nothing more; the rest waits. When the reader asks about a word, answer that question at their depth, and only then offer to go on. Read how inquisitive they are: a reader asking about every second term needs more ground before the next part, and you should say so plainly rather than press on. The goal is that the reader understands the current state and every word in front of them, not that the serial finishes. When they say continue, deliver the next part with its masthead and nothing else.

**The cold open stays.** The masthead exists so the prose does not have to orient anyone. Part 1 still opens on a fact, a scene, a person or a number, and the last part still takes the nearest exit; the frame carries the orientation and the reader's own eye carries the rest. Everything a serial's writer must not do to make the text feel guided (announce, preview, gloss the reader's experience back to them) is done once, mechanically, by the masthead instead.

If the piece is being written through `writing-shop`, the whole serial is written and edited before part 1 is shown: the structure advisor rules on the lead of part 1 and the exit of the last part, the editor holds every part to the vocabulary rule, the check holds every concrete, and the gate runs on the whole file. The reader sees the finished serial one part at a time, and the conversation between parts is the writer's, in the voice.
