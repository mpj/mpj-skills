# serial

An agent deep in a context assumes its reader is in the same context and has read everything. Then it delivers in one block, so the reader stops a few paragraphs in with a question and has to type it underneath the whole thing. In conversation they would have said: wait, I haven't understood this yet, explain that first. The serial is that stop, built into the form, and it is gradual on purpose: nothing is written ahead of the reader, and what comes next is shaped by how they understood what came before. The reader's own words are the input. Generating every part up front and showing them one at a time was tried and ruled out (2026-09-12); that is a wall of text with a curtain in front of it.

There is no short form and no long form. Everything the shop explains starts at serial 0 and continues exactly as far as the reader needs, which may be no further.

## A session, in order

**1. The map.** One structure consultation per session, not one per piece. The head writer pitches a **concept map**: the terms the explanation will lean on as nodes, each with a one-line plain definition and a candidate concrete, and "must be grounded before" edges between them; and the **pillar sentence** for serial 0. The structure advisor counsels on the order the reader will meet the terms in, on the pillar, and on the exit; the writer pushes back; the advisor's last word settles it. The settled map is the spec every later part is held to (the vocabulary rule below is a graph invariant on it), and it is revisable after the interview: a node the reader already holds is pruned or becomes a footing.

**2. The interview.** Before anything is explained, the **interviewer** (Brian Lamb's seat; charter in `writing-shop`'s `references/advisors.md`) asks three to five probes keyed to the map's nodes and to things the reader already said or uses: a file, a library, a habit. One clause each, no preamble, no praise, no explaining inside the question. The reader may describe their understanding, say "I don't know" (a complete and welcome answer), or say "I know this well". A claim of knowledge is never second-guessed; it is followed by the sharpest possible inquiry into what "this" is, because the thing the reader knows well is the footing the explanation will build on: if you know `map`, you can be taught the monad, and a developer who has chained promises has done monadic work without the word. The reader may ask the interviewer anything mid-probe and get a straight answer; the seat never withholds to make a point.

**3. The learner ledger.** The **assessor**, a plain seat with no author voice and never a Claude model, classifies each answer into one of five routes (correct with a sound account; correct but too brief to judge; correct with flawed reasoning; wrong; and no claim made, which is where "I don't know" goes), and keeps the ledger: for each node, its state, and the reader's own words as evidence. "Too brief to judge" gets one follow-up question, never a diagnosis. The ledger is internal and is never shown to the reader; the whole point of the form is less on the page, and a new concept called a learner ledger on the page would be the opposite. It drives the next part.

**4. Serial 0, the pillar.** The first delivery is a dense, straightforward expert statement of the thing with no pedagogy at all: the field's own terms, no analogy, no scene ("a monad is a monoid in the category of endofunctors", never "a monad is like a burrito"). If the reader already understands, this is enough; if not, it is a unit test of their understanding and a pillar in the distance every later part walks toward, each one rung down the ladder of abstraction from it. How it is made (ruled after an adversarial debate, 2026-09-12; the record is in the provenance): the pillar is argued inside the map consultation, so it gets exactly one reviewed argument and no extra call; where a recognized canonical formulation covers the thing at the needed scope, serial 0 uses it verbatim and attributed, and the writer only selects it; otherwise the writer's compression stands, and the writer's single call after the last word only formats the settled pillar and a TERMINOLOGY block drawn from the map's own definitions, in plain declarative register. Before delivery it passes a root-conformance gate inside the veridicality check: every term in it is an exact map node, every relation is stated or deliberately omitted without changing scope, the TERMINOLOGY block carries no new commitment, and the checker returns a disposition per clause; a failure blocks delivery and triggers one constrained rewrite.

**5. Parts on demand.** After serial 0, and after every part, the reader replies. The next part is generated then, and not before: one rung down, to the deepest node the ledger shows ungrounded, built from the nodes the reader holds well. Each part goes through the shop before delivery, per part: the editor's letter and the writer's reply, the tightening pass every second part (it hunts a fault that accumulates), the veridicality check with the conformance gate, the lint gate. When the ledger shows a misconception, the next part is a **detour** that meets it head-on and says so in its masthead; detours are not failures, they are the parts the form exists for, and there is no limit on them. The goal is that the reader can show they understand, in their own formulations, and that the agent has looked for the clues that they do not.

**6. Between parts.** The interviewer asks the follow-up that checks whether the part landed ("That part leaned on join. Say back what join did." "Where did it lose you?"), the assessor updates the ledger, and any question the reader asks is answered at their depth before anything else moves. Read how inquisitive they are: a reader asking about every second term needs more ground before the next rung, and the writer says so plainly rather than pressing on.

**7. The exit.** The serial ends when the ledger shows the map grounded to the depth the reader wanted, or when the reader stops. The last part takes the nearest exit; no summary coda in the prose, the checkpoint block does that job.

**Nothing is generated ahead of the reader.** Pre-generating likely next parts while the reader reads was proposed and ruled out (2026-09-12): it is a speculative cache built before anyone has looked at usage patterns, and pre-work that leaks is the wall of text this form exists to prevent. If a predictable next move ever shows in the record, revisit it with the data.

## Vocabulary

Each part introduces at most three new terms or names, each a node of the map. Each is defined in plain words at its first use, before anything leans on it, and no term is used in a part that was not grounded in an earlier part or marked in the ledger as held by the reader. The definitions are the specification, not clutter; an editor may sharpen them and may not cut them. The defining occurrence of each term is set in bold, once, and never again (the checkpoint block and the ground line list their terms plain): a reader scanning back for the bold finds where the word was introduced, and the ground line uses the same spelling. When a part iterates over named things, each name is bold at its first appearance in the list. Italics in the prose stay the writer's, for stress. That is the whole typographic budget; a page where everything is emphasised has no emphasis.

## The masthead

Above every part, outside the prose and not counted as prose, a fixed frame for the reader who just landed from another tab and needs to know in one glance what this is, who it is for, and where they stand. Four slots: a **title**, two to five words, a name a reader could pick out of a list of twenty documents, never a summary and never a name with an explainer after a colon; a **standfirst**, one sentence under twenty words saying what this document is and what it is about, so plainly a stranger understands it, never a teaser; a **position line**, "Part N of ?", the date, and who wrote it, which is the shop and never the anchor author (a run on 2026-09-12 put the anchor author's name in that slot and the check blocked delivery: an anchor is a fact about how the text was made, a byline is a claim about who wrote it, and no living author's name belongs on a document he did not write), with serial 0 as Part 0, ordinals clear and the denominator unknown until the serial ends, since detours are parts and take the next ordinal (a detour's position line says so: "Part 4 of ?, a detour"); and from Part 1 on a **ground line**, "So far:" followed by the terms already grounded, a bare list of nouns, mirroring the checkpoint blocks. The writer writes the masthead, in the voice, under the same rules as the prose. A masthead written by the relay in its own register is the first thing the reader will call machine-made, and one was.

## The checkpoint

The end of every part, and it must be self-descriptive: a reader who has never met the form should see on sight what it is and what it wants from him. A closing paragraph in the writer's voice was tried first and ruled out by the reader on 2026-09-12: it read as an unexplained inset, and it was not clear what it was for. The model is the end of a chapter in David Deutsch's *The Beginning of Infinity*, from the page the reader supplied: labelled blocks, each label a bold heading in capitals on its own line, the content beneath it. Three blocks, in this order, outside the prose and not counted as prose:

**TERMINOLOGY.** Each term the part introduced, in italics at the start of its own entry, followed by its definition in plain words, a sentence or two, as Deutsch sets them (*Natural numbers* The whole numbers 1, 2, 3 and so on.). This is the glossary the reader scans back to, and the ground line's list mirrors it, plain; a definition here may be sharper than the prose's, never looser. The italics do not break the term rule: the block is outside the prose, and the prose keeps its budget (bold once at the defining occurrence, italics for the writer's one stressed word).

**SUMMARY.** A short paragraph, one to three sentences, stating what the part established, so a reader who stops here knows what he holds. A statement of what was said, never a preview of what comes next; a preview is signposting and the tightening seat may cut it.

**CONTINUE OR ASK.** One line in the writer's voice, varied from part to part: say continue, or name any term above to have it explained at your depth before anything else moves. **The label is the exact set of moves the reader has, and nothing else.** When a part hands the reader an action as well (run the experiment, apply the change, choose between two things), the label names it: CONTINUE, ASK OR RUN. When the serial has reached its floor and there is no next rung, CONTINUE comes off the label: ASK, or ASK OR RUN. A move offered in the line beneath the label that the label does not name is a hidden option, and the reader called one out on 2026-09-12 ("if it's actually continue, ask, or run, then it should be clear"). The line under the label may not preview the next part either; that is signposting, and the tightening seat may cut it.

Deutsch's page carries a further block between the first two, the meanings of the book's title encountered in the chapter; it tracks that book's through-line and has no standing place in a serial. A piece with a carried through-line term of its own may add such a block, labelled after that term, and otherwise leaves it out.

The blocks are the reader's specification, like the masthead: an editor may sharpen a definition and may not cut one, and the tightening seat may not touch a label. The prose above them stays clean of orientation for the same reason the masthead exists: the frame carries it.

## Parts

120 to 220 words of prose each; serial 0 is shorter and is exempt. The masthead above and the checkpoint block below are outside the prose and not counted. Part 1 and every later part open on a fact, a scene, a person or a number, as the writer's standing prompt says; serial 0 alone opens on the statement itself. Assume the reader has read nothing: not the document you are explaining, not the earlier conversation, not the file you just wrote. They are arriving cold, holding a sandwich.

## Seats and their families

The head writer, the structure advisor, the precision editor and the tightening advisor are the shop's, as seated in `writing-shop`. The serial adds two: the interviewer, an anchored seat (Brian Lamb, with one Studs Terkel passage among the exemplars; charter and anchor sources in `references/advisors.md`), and the assessor, a plain seat with no author voice. Every prose seat and the assessor sit on a non-Claude model, the interviewer and the assessor on DeepSeek V4 Pro by default; the veridicality check is the only seat that may run on Claude or mirror the orchestrating agent. **Anchoring machinery runs once per session; forks share the prefix.** A session opens exactly one anchored conversation per seat, and every later call from that seat, the interviewer's follow-ups and the writer's parts included, continues it; nothing is re-anchored and no seat is opened twice.
