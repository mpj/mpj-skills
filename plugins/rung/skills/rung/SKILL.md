---
name: rung
description: Explain a thing to one person one rung at a time, shaped by what they already know. An interviewer probes before anything is explained; a dense expert statement stands as the pillar; every later part is written on demand from the reader's own words, one rung down the ladder of abstraction, with a detour whenever a misconception shows. The prose is written in an anchored voice by seats that argue by letter, checked against the world, and run through a mechanical gate. Use when someone will read the output as an explanation rather than glance at it as status, when they have said a reply overwhelmed them, or when they said "wait, I haven't understood this yet". The same voice writes a document when one is wanted.

# rung

This is a way to hold a discussion with one person that leaves them understanding something. It is a conversation with a shape, and the shape exists because of two failures that keep happening without it.

The first is the wall of text. An agent deep in a context assumes its reader is in the same context and has read everything, so it delivers in one block. The reader stops a few paragraphs in with a question and has to type it underneath the whole thing. In conversation they would have said: wait, I haven't understood this yet, explain that first. Every rule here about writing on demand is that stop, built into the form.

The second is the register. A model told to write like an author writes an essay *about* that author, in its own voice. Nine voices were auditioned that way on real material and all nine came back sounding like the same machine wearing nine hats. What fixed it was the author's own sentences: three verbatim passages in context, and the instruction to continue them. Anchored prose then carried an invented "eighteen months ago" past five editors, because every editor checked terms against a sheet and nobody checked the world. And prose that passed every mechanical gate was still handed back by its reader with one word, *sirligt* (Swedish: ornate, over-elegant), for sentences that announce the shape of the text instead of carrying it. The seats below are the answers to those three failures, in the order they were learned.

**This produces understanding, and sometimes it also produces a document.** The conversation is the thing. When a piece of writing is wanted and will be kept (a pull request body, a retrospective, an explainer someone else will read cold), the same voice and the same seats write it, at the fuller setting described under "What runs when". Nothing here builds web pages or files of any other kind; the output is prose in the conversation.

## A session, in order

**1. The map.** One structure consultation per session, not one per part. The head writer pitches a **concept map**: the terms the explanation will lean on as nodes, each with a one-line plain definition and a candidate concrete, and "must be grounded before" edges between them; and the **pillar sentence** that opens the explanation. The structure advisor counsels on the order the reader will meet the terms in, on the pillar, and on the exit; the writer pushes back; the advisor's last word settles it. The settled map is the spec every later part is held to (the vocabulary rule below is a graph invariant on it), and it is revisable after the interview: a node the reader already holds is pruned or becomes a footing.

**2. The interview.** Before anything is explained, the **interviewer** (Brian Lamb's seat; charter in `references/advisors.md`) asks three to five probes keyed to the map's nodes and to things the reader already said or uses: a file, a library, a habit. One clause each, no preamble, no praise, no explaining inside the question. The reader may describe their understanding, say "I don't know" (a complete and welcome answer), or say "I know this well". A claim of knowledge is never second-guessed; it is followed by the sharpest possible inquiry into what "this" is, because the thing the reader knows well is the footing the explanation will build on: if you know `map`, you can be taught the monad, and a developer who has chained promises has done monadic work without the word. The reader may ask the interviewer anything mid-probe and get a straight answer; the seat never withholds to make a point.

**3. The learner ledger.** The **assessor**, a plain seat with no author voice and never a Claude model, classifies each answer into one of five routes (correct with a sound account; correct but too brief to judge; correct with flawed reasoning; wrong; and no claim made, which is where "I don't know" goes), and keeps the ledger: for each node, its state, and the reader's own words as evidence. "Too brief to judge" gets one follow-up question, never a diagnosis. The ledger is internal and is never shown to the reader; the whole point of the form is less on the page, and a new concept called a learner ledger on the page would be the opposite. It drives the next part.

**4. The pillar, delivered as Part 0.** The first delivery is a dense, straightforward expert statement of the thing with no pedagogy at all: the field's own terms, no analogy, no scene ("a monad is a monoid in the category of endofunctors", never "a monad is like a burrito"). If the reader already understands, this is enough and the session is over. If not, it is a unit test of their understanding and the pillar in the distance every later part walks toward, each one rung down the ladder of abstraction from it. How it is made (ruled after an adversarial debate, 2026-09-12; the record is in the provenance): the pillar is argued inside the map consultation, so it gets exactly one reviewed argument and no extra call; where a recognized canonical formulation covers the thing at the needed scope, the pillar uses it verbatim and attributed, and the writer only selects it; otherwise the writer's compression stands, and the writer's single call after the last word only formats the settled pillar and a TERMINOLOGY block drawn from the map's own definitions, in plain declarative register. Before delivery it passes a root-conformance gate inside the veridicality check: every term in it is an exact map node, every relation is stated or deliberately omitted without changing scope, the TERMINOLOGY block carries no new commitment and carries an entry for every term the prose uses under the same spelling, every map node the pillar's scope commits to reaches the prose under its settled name or is named as deliberately omitted, and the checker returns a disposition per clause; a failure blocks delivery and triggers one constrained rewrite, which the writer makes, because the gate reports and never edits.

**5. Parts on demand.** After the pillar, and after every part, the reader replies. The next part is written then, and not before: one rung down, to the deepest node the ledger shows ungrounded, built from the nodes the reader holds well. When the ledger shows a misconception, the next part is a **detour** that meets it head-on and says so in its position line; detours are not failures, they are the parts the form exists for, and there is no limit on them. The goal is that the reader can show they understand, in their own formulations, and that the agent has looked for the clues that they do not.

**6. Between parts.** The interviewer's follow-up ("That part leaned on join. Say back what join did." "Where did it lose you?") is written after the part and delivered inside its checkpoint, so checking whether a part landed costs no turn of its own. The assessor routes whatever comes back, and any question the reader asks is answered at their depth before anything else moves. Read how inquisitive they are: a reader asking about every second term needs more ground before the next rung, and the writer says so plainly rather than pressing on. When a reader makes no claim for several parts running, the ledger has nothing to update and the map's own edges decide the order; say so rather than implying the ledger chose.

**7. The exit.** The session ends when the ledger shows the map grounded to the depth the reader wanted, or when the reader stops. The last part takes the nearest exit; no summary coda in the prose, the checkpoint block does that job.

**Nothing is written ahead of the reader.** Writing every part up front and showing them one at a time is a wall of text with a curtain in front of it, and it was ruled out on 2026-09-12. Pre-generating likely next parts while the reader reads was ruled out the same day: it is a speculative cache built before anyone has looked at usage patterns, and pre-work that leaks is the wall of text this form exists to prevent. If a predictable next move ever shows in the record, revisit it with the data.

## What runs when

The interview, the ledger and the writer are cheap. The letters are not: a part that goes through the whole shop costs several model calls and several minutes between the reader's "continue" and the next screen of text. So the machinery runs at two settings, and an agent picks by asking one question. **Is this read once in the conversation, or kept?**

**Every turn, always.** The map consultation (once per session), the interview, the ledger, the writer in the anchored voice, and the lint gate. The pillar additionally takes the root-conformance gate inside the veridicality check, every time, because it is the one delivery with no rung above it to correct it.

**When it will be kept, or when its facts matter.** The precision editor's letter and the writer's item-by-item reply; the tightening pass, on every second part (the fault it hunts accumulates); the veridicality check against the world on every concrete. A part that carries dates, counts, names, quotes or attributions gets the check whatever else it gets, because a false concrete does not care how casual the conversation was. The full protocol, letter by letter, is in `references/protocol.md`.

The reader can ask for the fuller setting by name at any point, and an agent about to write something the reader will paste somewhere else reaches for it without being asked.

## Whose words reach the reader

**The shop does the writing, and what the shop writes is delivered to the reader as it came back.** The seats are why this skill exists; an agent that paraphrases their prose into its own has run the machinery and thrown away the output. A part, a pillar, a probe and a checkpoint block reach the reader verbatim.

The orchestrating agent still has things to say. It fills in, it reports what the run did, it answers a question at the reader's depth, it says what it checked and what it found. All of that is allowed and often needed. What is not allowed is letting the two run together, because then every sentence the reader meets is in the agent's register and the seats might as well not have sat.

**So mark the boundary.** Say plainly where the agent's own writing stops and a seat's begins, and say which seat wrote what. A reader who cannot tell the difference is reading one voice, and it is the voice this skill exists to get out from under. A reader who asks "did you write that or did the shop" and gets a straight answer is the test; a run on 2026-09-13 failed it, when a whole report of the shop's own findings was written by the orchestrating agent in the agent's register with four probes from the interviewer embedded in it unmarked.

## Vocabulary

Each part introduces at most three new terms or names, each a node of the map. Each is defined in plain words at its first use, before anything leans on it, and no term is used in a part that was not grounded in an earlier part or marked in the ledger as held by the reader. The definitions are the specification, not clutter; an editor may sharpen them and may not cut them. The defining occurrence of each term is set in bold, once, and never again (the checkpoint block lists its terms plain): a reader scanning back for the bold finds where the word was introduced, and the checkpoint uses the same spelling. When a part iterates over named things, each name is bold at its first appearance in the list. Italics in the prose stay the writer's, for stress. That is the whole typographic budget; a page where everything is emphasised has no emphasis.

**A term is held when the reader has shown he holds it, and never because he wrote it.** Do not prune a node on the grounds that the reader authored the document defining the term, introduced the word, or named the thing. The reader ruled on 2026-09-13, after a part skipped three terms on exactly that reasoning and he asked for all three back: "yes i wrote it but i also write so many things with ai that i cannot possibly keep tabs on or pay attention to all the things i've generated. i need to be reminded and the question of whether it's my authorship is not as relevant as it used to be back in the day." The terms that need giving back are the hard ones and the rare ones, and a word like *monad* or *ontic* is not in the hand for having been used once: "i need to be reminded of even when i brought them in, just because it's been a few weeks and i've been mingled up in another project at work". A node is grounded by the ledger or by a claim the reader makes in this session, and by nothing else. Where an agent is tempted to prune on authorship, the interviewer probes it instead, which costs one clause; the guess costs the reader a part written over his head.

**The pillar is exempt from the three-term limit, and its prose carries no bold.** It carries the whole vocabulary by construction, and its TERMINOLOGY block, sitting directly beneath it, is where those terms are defined. Bold exists so a reader scanning back up the page can find where a word was introduced. In the pillar every word is introduced, so there is no earlier occurrence to point at, and the block underneath does that job better, with each term in italics against its definition. Marking eight terms in one dense sentence emphasises the sentence, and a page where everything is emphasised has no emphasis.

The exemption stops there. The pillar's terms are still the map's nodes under the map's own names, and the conformance gate holds it to that, including to naming a node the pillar leans on instead of reaching for a stand-in phrase. Ruled 2026-09-13 on the evidence of two runs; the record is in the provenance.

## The position line

Above every part, outside the prose and not counted as prose, two thin lines and no more:

* A **position line**: "Part N of ?", with the pillar as Part 0, the denominator unknown until the session ends, and a detour marked as one ("Part 4 of ?, a detour") since detours take the next ordinal.
* From Part 1 on, a **question line**: the question this part answers, or the pair of questions it answers, put as plainly as it can be put. The pillar takes the position line alone, since the question it answers is the session's.

That is the whole frame for a conversation. The position line says where the reader stands and the question line says what this part is for.

**The question line may be opaque before the part and must not be opaque after it.** The test the reader set on 2026-09-13, replacing a line that listed the terms grounded so far: a reader who holds the terminology and has just finished the part should be able to see that the question was answered. So it is written as a question and never as a topic, it is not a summary of the part (SUMMARY does that) and never a preview of the next one, and a part that cannot be reduced to one question or a pair of them is doing more than one part's work and should be split. A line that merely recites what has already been covered tells a reader who was there nothing he does not have. A title, a standfirst and a byline above every reply make a turn look like a published page, which is the thing this form is trying not to be.

**The full masthead belongs on a document, and only there.** When the output is something the reader will keep or hand on, it opens with four slots instead of two: a **title**, two to five words, a name a reader could pick out of a list of twenty documents, never a summary and never a name with an explainer after a colon; a **standfirst**, one sentence under twenty words saying what this document is and what it is about, so plainly a stranger understands it, never a teaser; then the position line and the question line as above. The writer writes it, in the voice, under the same rules as the prose. A masthead written by the orchestrating agent in its own register is the first thing the reader will call machine-made, and one was.

**A byline names the shop and never the anchor author.** A run on 2026-09-12 put the anchor author's name in that slot and the check blocked delivery: an anchor is a fact about how the text was made, a byline is a claim about who wrote it, and no living author's name belongs on a document he did not write.

## The checkpoint

The end of every part, and it must be self-descriptive: a reader who has never met the form should see on sight what it is and what it wants from him. A closing paragraph in the writer's voice was tried first and ruled out by the reader on 2026-09-12: it read as an unexplained inset, and it was not clear what it was for. The model is the end of a chapter in David Deutsch's *The Beginning of Infinity*, from the page the reader supplied: labelled blocks, each label a bold heading in capitals on its own line, the content beneath it. Three blocks, in this order, outside the prose and not counted as prose:

**TERMINOLOGY.** Each term the part introduced, in italics at the start of its own entry, followed by its definition in plain words, a sentence or two, as Deutsch sets them (*Natural numbers* The whole numbers 1, 2, 3 and so on.). This is the glossary the reader scans back to; a definition here may be sharper than the prose's, never looser. The italics do not break the term rule: the block is outside the prose, and the prose keeps its budget (bold once at the defining occurrence, italics for the writer's one stressed word).

**SUMMARY.** A short paragraph, one to three sentences, stating what the part established, so a reader who stops here knows what he holds. A statement of what was said, never a preview of what comes next; a preview is signposting and the tightening seat may cut it. The pillar takes no SUMMARY block, because the pillar is the summary of itself.

**I UNDERSTAND, ASK OR GO ON.** Three moves, and they are three because "continue" was one word doing two jobs. A reader who types it may mean he has the part, or may mean he has not and wants the next one anyway, and the assessor cannot tell which from the word. The reader ruled on 2026-09-13, after three replies in a row the ledger could not hold: "it's a little ambiguous what 'continue' means. perhaps we should just use the term 'i understand' (as in 'i understand part one') and then ask questions about it to fill in."

* **I understand.** A claim of knowledge, scoped to the part. The skill already knows what to do with one: it is trusted and never second-guessed, and it is followed at once by the sharpest inquiry into what "this" is. So this move turns the cheapest thing a reader can type into the evidence the ledger runs on.
* **Ask.** Name any term above and have it explained at your depth before anything else moves.
* **Go on.** No claim, no follow-up, next part. It exists so nobody has to claim understanding in order to keep moving, and the ledger takes it as the fifth route, no claim made, which is what it honestly is.

Under the label, two lines and no more: the writer's own line naming the moves, varied from part to part, and then **the interviewer's one question about this part**, which rides here instead of costing a turn of its own. The reader answers it when he says "I understand", ignores it when he says "go on", and either way the next part is the first thing he sees after he replies. **The label is the exact set of moves the reader has, and nothing else.** When a part hands the reader an action as well (run the experiment, apply the change, choose between two things), the label names it: I UNDERSTAND, ASK, GO ON OR RUN. When the session has reached its floor and there is no next rung, the two forward moves come off it: ASK, or ASK OR RUN. A move offered in the line beneath the label that the label does not name is a hidden option, and the reader called one out on 2026-09-12 ("if it's actually continue, ask, or run, then it should be clear"). The line under the label may not preview the next part either; that is signposting, and the tightening seat may cut it.

Deutsch's page carries a further block between the first two, the meanings of the book's title encountered in the chapter; it tracks that book's through-line and has no standing place here. A session with a carried through-line term of its own may add such a block, labelled after that term, and otherwise leaves it out.

The blocks are the reader's specification, like the position line: an editor may sharpen a definition and may not cut one, and the tightening seat may not touch a label. The prose above them stays clean of orientation for the same reason the position line exists: the frame carries it.

## Parts

120 to 220 words of prose each; the pillar is shorter and is exempt. The lines above and the checkpoint below are outside the prose and not counted. Part 1 and every later part open on a fact, a scene, a person or a number, as the writer's standing prompt says; the pillar alone opens on the statement itself. Assume the reader has read nothing: not the document you are explaining, not the earlier conversation, not the file you just wrote. They are arriving cold, holding a sandwich.

## The seats

The **head writer** writes everything, in one voice. The seated voice is Joel Spolsky, chosen by audition; the standing prompt is in `references/head-writer.md`, written in his voice, with the standing law the editors accumulated (one thing, one name; machine strings wear code face; a concrete that might be false is worse than none). The **structure advisor**, William Zinsser, is consulted once per session on the map, the pillar and the exit. The **precision editor**, Martin Fowler, runs the line pass: definitions, one name per concept, distinctions that matter, and the order and marking of terms, under Kernighan's boundary that the code font marks the string and the deadpan is the writer's business. The **tightening advisor**, Orwell with his fifth rule suspended, runs last and lightly: at most twelve proposals, cuts and short-for-long swaps only, hunting the ornate and the signpost. The **interviewer**, Brian Lamb, probes the reader before anything is explained and asks the one follow-up after each part. The **assessor**, a plain seat with no author voice, keeps the ledger of what the reader has shown he knows. The **veridicality check** is requested by the writer and run by an agent with tools against whatever the claims are about: the repository, the API, the file, the calendar. The **gate** is four mechanical rules run on the finished text. Charters for the advisors and the interviewer, the assessor's rules, and the bench of candidates who lost their seats and why, are in `references/advisors.md`.

Every seat is set up the same way: it fetches three or more verbatim passages of its author's published prose, 300 words or longer with the punctuation intact, derives a ten-line bible of mechanical traits from what it actually read, receives a plain ground-truth sheet of every fact the text may assert, and has the hard rules restated in its own brief, because inherited context is not enough. Seats never speak to each other; every letter passes through the orchestrating agent verbatim, and that agent is also where the facts get checked and the rulings logged. The letters are not theatre: the pushback puts trade-offs where the reader can see them, the advisor's last word makes a settled structure the revision can be held to, and staying in character keeps each seat inside its anchored texture instead of drifting back to the model's default. Collapsing the exchange into "just edit it" reintroduces the single-pass trade of style against correctness that the two-pass shape exists to avoid.

**Anchoring machinery runs once per session; forks share the prefix.** A session opens exactly one anchored conversation per seat, and every later call from that seat, the interviewer's follow-ups and the writer's parts included, continues it; nothing is re-anchored and no seat is opened twice.

## Seating the models

**Every prose seat and the assessor sit on a non-Claude model.** The head writer sits on DeepSeek V4 Pro, always and explicitly, by the reader's ruling of 2026-09-12: this exists to get prose out from under one model's fingerprint and mannerisms, and a Claude writer, however well it reads, puts the voice back under the family the reader is trying to escape. The veridicality check is the only seat that may mirror the orchestrating agent or run on Claude; that check is against the world, not the prose. Never let a model rule on its own family's prose without knowing the price: an in-family editor scores signposting as a virtue, and when writer and editor shared a model the editor caught a sixth of what a cross-family editor caught and echoed the writer's own error. The measured seating, dated because it will go stale, is in `references/model-selection.md`.

## What this knows about itself

The gate cannot see the fault the tightening seat exists for. Its four rules (em dashes and their spaced-hyphen cousins; the "not X, but Y" reversal frame; a banned lexicon; probable rule-of-three endings) are lexical, and discourse signposting has no remarkable word in it. `references/lint-gate.md` has the rules as patterns any agent can run; do not extend it with signpost regexes, which undercount a reader by about four times and teach the model to announce without the tell words. A deliberate exemption (a quoted lexicon, a term of art, a title with a dash in it) is logged with the text, never edited away.

The line pass does not see the world. In three runs the editor caught none, four and three of the false concretes in a draft; the check caught nine, nine and eighteen. Terms and mechanisms are the editor's; dates and counts and attributions are the check's; and renaming a term of art is a factual act, because "false concretes" rewritten as "invented numbers" turned four true sentences false without touching a number. The ground-truth sheet is itself a concrete: it ages between runs, and "today" is the assumption nobody states, so pin the date from the clock in every brief.

Per-part editing does not see the whole. A validation run on 2026-09-12 found that a fresh editor reading four delivered parts at once caught what three per-part letters had missed: one term defined one way in one checkpoint and another way two parts later, and a later part quietly dropping one of two distinctions an earlier part had established. Neither is visible from inside a single part. When a session has produced something that will be kept, the whole of it gets one read before it is handed over.

A seat that cannot lose an argument is not a seat. Every seat here has lost one to the writer on the merits, and those were the arguments worth having. A writer that accepts every proposal is ventriloquism, and one run produced exactly that: twenty-two of twenty-two accepted, including a cut that made a sentence stop being English. The provenance of every choice above, including the runs that went wrong, is in `references/provenance.md`.
